TFSA over-contribution penalty: how the 1% a month is calculated

Published 2026-10-25 · Checked against official sources 2026-10-25 · All guides

Go over your TFSA room and the CRA taxes the excess at 1% per month, counted on the highest excess you held in each month. Because every month counts in full, the date you fix it matters more than most people expect.

What counts as an over-contribution

Your TFSA room for a year is the annual limit plus unused room carried forward from earlier years plus withdrawals you made in the previous year, minus what you have already contributed (see the CRA's room page). If your contributions in a year push past that number, the part above it is an excess TFSA amount.

The CRA lists three common ways it happens: miscalculating your room, re-contributing a withdrawal in the same year without enough room, and contributing to several TFSAs without tracking the combined total. The room belongs to you, not to each account, so two TFSAs at two banks share one limit.

How the 1% tax is calculated

The tax is 1% of the highest excess amount in your account during each month it remains. Two details trip people up:

  • Whole months. A single day in a month is a full month of tax. Fixing it on the 30th costs the same as fixing it on the 1st.
  • Highest, not average. If you remove part of the excess mid-month, that month is still taxed on the larger figure. The smaller figure applies from the next month.

The CRA's own examples show it. Over-contribute $2,000 in June and withdraw it in September and you pay $20 for each of June, July, August and September: $80. Withdraw it later in June and you owe $20. Over-contribute $6,000 in August, withdraw $4,000 in mid-September and the last $2,000 in October: $60 for August, $60 for September and $20 for October, which is $140.

Worked example: the same-year re-deposit Sam contributed the full $7,000 for 2026 in January, took out $5,000 in March for a car repair, and put $5,000 back in June, thinking the withdrawal freed up the room. It did not: withdrawals are added back to your room on January 1 of the next year, not straight away. In June Sam is $5,000 over.
  • Fixed in June: 1% × $5,000 = $50
  • Fixed in September (June, July, August, September): 4 × $50 = $200
  • Left in the account the whole year: it keeps costing $50 every month

What delay costs

1% a month sounds small, but it is a flat charge on the excess for every month it stays. The table shows the tax for different excess sizes and fix times (excess held at one level the whole time).

What to do first

  1. Withdraw the excess from the account as soon as you know. The CRA says to do this "as soon as possible" and not to wait for a notice. You do not need to call the CRA; your institution reports the withdrawal.
  2. Work out the highest excess in each month it was there (our TFSA room calculator shows your room by year, and your own deposit and withdrawal dates give the rest).
  3. File a TFSA Return (Form RC243) with Schedule A (RC243-SCH-A) and pay what you owe by June 30 of the year after the tax applies. Tax from 2026 is due June 30, 2027. You can submit through "Submit documents" in your CRA account.
  4. Ask for relief if it was a genuine mistake. The CRA may waive or cancel the tax if it arose from a reasonable error and you withdrew the excess to fix it. You request this in a letter explaining what happened.

The CRA checks TFSA contributions once a year and typically notifies people in late spring. Institutions report to the CRA by the end of February, so an excess can sit for several months before you hear about it, and the 1% meter runs the whole time.

Deliberate over-contributions

The CRA warns that an excess caused by a deliberate over-contribution may be taxed as an "advantage" at the 100% advantage rate instead of 1% a month. The Income Tax Act defines a deliberate over-contribution in s.207.01. An honest miscalculation is the 1% case; knowingly contributing far beyond your room to exploit tax-free growth is the case to avoid.

How to avoid it

  • Treat the CRA account figure as a starting point: it is updated once a year in spring and does not include this year's deposits. Compare it with your institution's records.
  • Keep a log of every deposit and withdrawal by date and institution. A TFSA transfer made directly between institutions is not a contribution; withdrawing and redepositing yourself is.
  • Do not re-contribute a withdrawal in the same calendar year unless you still have unused room without counting it.

Frequently asked questions

Is the TFSA over-contribution tax 1% of the whole balance?

No. It is 1% of the highest excess amount in each month the excess stays in the account, not of your total TFSA balance.

If I withdraw the excess on the last day of the month, do I still owe that month?

Yes. The CRA's own example confirms the 1% applies for the month of the over-contribution even if you remove it later in the same month.

When do I pay the TFSA over-contribution tax?

File Form RC243 with Schedule A and pay by June 30 of the year after the year the tax applies. For tax arising in 2026, that is June 30, 2027.

Can the CRA cancel the tax?

It may waive or cancel it if it arose from a reasonable error, looking at how much the transaction led to other tax and how much you withdrew to correct it. You send a letter through your CRA account or by mail.
